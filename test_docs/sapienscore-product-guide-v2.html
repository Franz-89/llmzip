Not just “online”

Most monitoring tools tell you one thing: is the device on or off. That’s useful. It’s also nowhere near enough. A game can be powered on and earning nothing because something inside it stopped — a stuck coin mech, a jammed dispenser, a relay that won’t fire. Let’s be honest about this part: arcade hardware is decades-old technology. It rarely raises its hand to say it’s broken. You usually only know by what stops happening.

So we ask a more honest question: is it actually doing what it should?

  • For every game: the PulseBox we install sits between the game, the coin acceptor, and the ticket dispenser — so we see every coin inserted, every payment, every game start, every ticket dispensed. We can’t know if the screen is cracked or the joystick is dead, and we won’t pretend otherwise. But we know when a machine that usually starts twenty games an hour suddenly starts zero. That pattern shows up immediately, and we tell you — instead of waiting for a customer complaint.
  • For every card terminal: the app is ours, on hardware we know. Is it running? Is the card reader responding? Is the payment SDK healthy? “Powered on” isn’t the same as “ready to take money,” and we know the difference.
  • For every local server: CPU, memory, disk, network, services. UPS battery level. Voltage. Connected USB devices. Everything that matters, watched continuously.

You verify, you don’t hope

Every transaction produces evidence. Every operation leaves a trail. Built that way on purpose.

A counter that’s wrong on purpose

Your collector hands you the cash from yesterday on Game 23. The mechanical counter on the machine says €840 worth of plays. The cash in the bag adds up to €620.

Two hundred and twenty euros short.

Did the collector skim? Or did the Nayax reader and the loyalty card reader together process €220 of cashless plays yesterday — both of which also spin the physical counter, because in most setups they share the same coin line into the game — meaning the counter was always going to be wrong by exactly that amount?

You don’t know. You sign the report. You make a note to “look into it.” Two weeks from now this will happen again, with different numbers.

This is what happens when an industry asks you to trust people for things that should produce evidence.

The collector might be honest. The reader might be miscounted. The fraud might be real, or the gap might just be physics. You can’t tell. And in a business where this happens hundreds of times a month, “you can’t tell” is expensive.

Why trust isn’t enough

Most operators run their business on trust. Trust the collector. Trust the cashier. Trust the prize counter staff. Trust the technician who logged a manual ticket adjustment. Trust the system to record what really happened.

Trust is fine until something goes wrong. Then trust gives you nothing.

You can’t fire someone on suspicion. You can’t recover stolen money on a hunch. You can’t tell your accountant “the gap is probably real — we’ll just absorb it” for the third quarter in a row. You need evidence.

Most systems don’t give you evidence. They give you reports. A report shows what was recorded. It doesn’t show whether what was recorded matches what actually happened. SapiensCore is built around the opposite assumption: everything important should produce its own evidence, automatically, with no one having to remember.

Cash you can prove

The Visa-spinning-the-counter problem from the opening of this chapter isn’t a small annoyance. It’s the reason most operators can’t tell whether they have a fraud problem or a counter problem. Until you separate the two, you have neither answer.

We solved it with hardware. The PulseBox is the only device connected directly to the game. Everything else — the coin acceptor, the Visa reader, the loyalty card reader, the ticket dispenser — runs through the PulseBox. Nothing reaches the game’s coin counter except the coin acceptor itself.

So when someone pays with a Visa card or a loyalty card, the PulseBox starts the game directly — without ever touching the coin counter. The mechanical counter only moves when a real physical coin is inserted. The math becomes clean: the counter reads X, the cash should equal X, and anything else is a real discrepancy worth investigating.

No reconciliation spreadsheets. No subtracting Nayax transactions from counter readings. No three-step formulas to estimate what the cash should have been. You either match, or you don’t.

Counting cash that doesn’t take your word for it

Collection day. The staff member opens the coin box and counts the cash. They photograph the mechanical counter with the PDA before resetting it. SapiensCore’s OCR reads the number from the photo. If the number the staff member types differs from the OCR reading, a notification fires immediately — before the cash even leaves the machine.

The photo is evidence. The OCR reading is evidence. The cash count is evidence. They all have to agree.

Two people, two moments — on purpose

SapiensCore separates the act of reading the counter from the act of counting the cash. They happen at different times, often by different people. The same person can’t silently massage both numbers to make them match, because they’re not both in the same person’s hands.

Cash that has to arrive where you sent it

Cash doesn’t finish its journey at the collection point. It moves to the on-site safe. Then to the bank. SapiensCore tracks every movement — who handled it, when, how much. If the cash doesn’t arrive at the bank within the expected window, an alert fires. Cash disappearing between “collected” and “deposited” used to be one of the easiest losses to hide. Now it produces an alarm.

Loyalty card operations you can audit

Every operation on a loyalty card produces a record: who did it, when, from which workstation, what the card’s state was before, what it became. No exceptions. No silent overrides. No “the system just did it” without a name attached.

Records alone aren’t evidence — patterns are. SapiensCore looks at operations across cards, across staff, across time, and flags the patterns that don’t look like real customer behavior.

The top-up that can’t happen without a real payment

Remember the 8:12 PM scene from the pain section — the employee typing “10” on the card terminal while loading a hundred euros of credit? Or marking the sale “paid by card” without ever touching the terminal at all? In SapiensCore, neither is possible.

When the counter system initiates a top-up, the exact amount is pushed automatically to the card terminal over WiFi. The employee doesn’t type it. They can’t override it. And there is no “paid by card” option without a real terminal confirmation coming back. The fraud surface collapses to zero. You can’t close a hole that’s already closed by design.

Recursive join chain analysis

Cards get merged. Balances move. That’s normal. It’s also one of the most-used vectors for internal fraud: balances quietly funneled from cards nobody’s watching into one card that gets used. Two levels deep, three levels deep — it’s hard to spot manually.

SapiensCore traces the entire chain. Card A joined into Card B joined into Card C joined into the target card. We follow it as far as it goes — no depth limit. At each step we show who performed the join, what balances moved, and how the source cards were used (or not used) before being merged. Patterns that would take a human two days of forensic spreadsheet work, SapiensCore reconstructs in seconds.

Recovery codes — designed against fraud, then watched anyway

When a customer inserts paper tickets at the Ticket Station without first tapping a card — or doesn’t tap one within 90 seconds — the station shows a short recovery code on screen and pushes it in real time to the counter. The customer walks over, the staff already sees the code waiting on the counter screen, selects it, taps “apply,” and the tickets land on a card (new or existing). The staff never types the ticket count. The system does. No room to round up “for the friend.”

It’s also why a passerby can’t tap their own card on the Ticket Station and walk off with someone else’s tickets. Once the tickets enter the recovery-code flow, only that specific code can claim them — and only at the counter.

But codes go unclaimed. Customers leave, customers forget, tourists never come back. Those expired codes are the fraud vector: quietly swept up later by staff and redeemed onto a personal card. A recovery code is meant to be used once, by the customer who earned the tickets. Finding 120 recovery codes redeemed onto a single loyalty card is not a customer pattern — it’s a staff-and-friend pattern. SapiensCore flags it the moment volume exceeds plausibility, cross-referenced with the staff member who applied each one.

Discounts that live on the card, not on a button

Most systems give employees a discount button. Anyone can press it. Nobody is accountable. End of the month, you see “discounts: way higher than expected,” with no way to know who or why.

SapiensCore inverts the design. Discounts live on the card as membership rules. Your employees have personal cards with employee discounts on them. Your partners have membership cards with the right partnership discounts. When the right card is used, the discount applies automatically — tracked, accountable, attributable to that specific card.

Discount buttons still exist for genuine edge cases (a one-off complaint resolution, a walk-in deal). But they’re rule-based, role-restricted, and every use logs the operator, timestamp, reason, and amount. The principle: the fewer times the button is pressed, the better your configuration is.

Tickets and prizes you can trust

The prize counter is the most fraud-prone moment in any venue. A customer wins, a number gets typed, a barcode gets scanned, a prize walks out the door. Whether the right number, the right barcode, and the right prize all matched up — you usually find out at end-of-month, if at all.

Manual ticket controls, per person, per card, per day

Manual ticket adjustments are sometimes necessary (a machine malfunction, a customer complaint, a correction). They’re also one of the easiest staff-side fraud vectors. SapiensCore lets you control them at every level:

  • Maximum tickets per single manual operation — per location
  • Maximum tickets per employee per day — specific people can be capped, or banned from manual ticket ops entirely
  • Maximum manual tickets per card per day — so even multiple staff can’t balloon one card
  • Every manual operation requires a comment — the reason becomes part of the record

Credit-VAT — the chapter your accountant should read

What VAT-postponed credit actually is, why almost nobody else offers it, and why it may be the single most important chapter in this document.

Every venue in this industry tops up loyalty cards. Customer pays €20, card receives €20 of credit (plus a bonus, maybe). The customer plays when they’re ready. Some credit gets spent. Some sits for months. Some never gets touched at all.

From the moment the top-up happens, almost every accounting system in the industry treats that €20 as revenue. VAT is calculated on it. Declared. Paid to the tax authority on your next cycle.

This is wrong. Not technically wrong — legally tolerated — but conceptually wrong. A top-up is not a sale. It’s a deposit. The customer is prepaying for a service they haven’t received. VAT should only be calculated and declared when that credit is actually spent — because that’s when an actual service or product is delivered.

The difference between “VAT at top-up” and “VAT at spend” sounds academic. It is not. It is money. Your money, sitting in your tax authority’s account instead of yours, because of credit your customers may never spend.

The test, in slow motion

Open your current loyalty card software. Find the total credit currently sitting on customer cards, unused. Let’s say it’s €100,000. Apply your local VAT rate — in Spain or Portugal, around 21-23%. That’s roughly €20,000 you’ve already paid to your tax authority.

On credit your customers haven’t used yet. Some of them never will. Some cards have been inactive for two years and aren’t coming back.

You can’t recover the VAT you’ve already paid on currently-unused credit. That money is gone. What you can do is stop paying it from this point forward, on every future top-up, across every location. Which is what Credit-VAT does.

How SapiensCore handles it

When Credit-VAT is enabled, SapiensCore separates the moment of top-up from the moment of taxation.

A €20 top-up loads €20 of credit onto the card. No VAT is calculated. No VAT is declared. Nothing is paid to the tax authority. Not yet.

VAT is calculated the moment that credit is actually consumed — on the exact amount consumed, at the correct VAT rate for the specific product or service. Game play: one rate. Food and drink: another. Birthday party hire: maybe another. Every transaction knows what service it’s paying for and applies the right rate, on the precise amount.

This sounds straightforward. It isn’t. Consider a normal customer:

  • Customer tops up €20. Receives €20 of credit plus, say, €5 of bonus (free).
  • Plays a game costing €1.80. The system consumes from bonus first — €1.80 from bonus, no credit touched, no VAT due. (The bonus was never taxable revenue in the first place.)
  • Next game, €1.80 again. Bonus is now €1.40. Bonus covers part, credit covers part. VAT is calculated on the part that came from credit, at the game-play rate.
  • Customer buys a snack with their card. Different VAT rate. Calculated correctly on the consumed amount, at the food rate.
  • Customer’s remaining credit expires after 18 months unused. The unused portion is accounted for under its own rules — not silently taxed at the wrong moment.

Every single transaction knows: how much credit was consumed, from which balance type, at what VAT rate, for what category of service. The system tracks all of this automatically. Per play. Per partial. Per customer. Per location.

The consumption priority that makes it work

The reason this all stays correct is that SapiensCore manages multiple balance types on every card and chooses automatically which to consume first. The order is designed for both customer fairness and your business:

  • Bonus and courtesy first. Free balances the customer was given are consumed before anything they actually paid for. Their own credit lasts longer.
  • Regular credit next. Credit that was already VAT-declared at top-up (in pre-Credit-VAT systems, or during a transition).
  • Credit-VAT last. The moment Credit-VAT is consumed, you become VAT-liable for that amount. So the system uses it only when no other balance is available. Your tax obligations stay at the legal minimum, automatically.

Staff never decide this. The customer never sees it. The system applies it on every transaction, transparently, and at the end of the month the books are simply correct.
