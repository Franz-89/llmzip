The hands-on guide: how to open a trace the right way, and how to drive it day to day — comments, the solution, checklists, costs and the people involved.

New to Trace? Read Trace — Overview & how to use it first for the big picture. This guide is the practical detail.

Creating a trace

Click New to open the create form. It is a short, guided sequence — you only fill what's relevant.

1. What is it about? (scope)

Pick the kind of thing the trace concerns: a Game, Kiosk, NUC, POS Terminal, Nayax, Reservation, Customer, Location, Payment, Cash, or Other. This is the scope, and it decides what you'll pick next.

2. Which one? (the entity)

Now choose the specific thing — the entity. How you pick depends on the scope:

  • Things tied to a location (a game, a NUC, a kiosk): choose the location first to narrow the list, then pick from it.
  • Larger sets (a customer, a payment, a terminal): just start typing to search by name, serial, code, etc.
  • Cash, Location and Other need no specific entity — skip straight on.

Why the entity matters. Attaching the trace to the real thing is what gives it context — anyone opening it knows exactly which machine, terminal or customer, and all the history against that entity is linked together. A trace without an entity is just a sticky note.

3. Describe it

Give it a short, clear title and, if it helps, a longer description. Because the context is already attached, the title can focus on the problem — "Note acceptor rejects €10" rather than "machine issue".

4. Classify

  • Type — Maintenance, Incident, Complaint, Request, Fraud, or Other.
  • Priority — Low, Normal, High, Critical.
  • Operational status — for hardware only: is the thing Operational, Degraded, or Offline?

5. Who & when

Optionally set an assignee (the one person responsible), a due date, and an effort estimate (how much work it is). None are required — you can add them later.

6. Optional: checklist & files

You can seed a starting checklist of steps and attach files (a photo of the fault, a document) right away.

Don't over-think it. A scope, an entity and a good title are enough to create a useful trace. Everything else can be filled in as the work goes.

The detail view

Click any card to open it. At the top you'll see the trace #number and title, and a row of controls to change the status, priority, assignee, due date and effort at any time. Click the title (the pencil) to rename it.

Just below, when the trace connects to others, a relationship bar shows its parent, blockers, what it blocks, and its sub-tasks — covered in Trace — Sub-tasks & dependencies. The rest of the work is organised into tabs.

Timeline — the conversation & history

The Timeline is where the work is recorded — your comments plus automatic entries (status changes and the like). The newest entry is at the top.

Writing

Use the composer to post a message — progress, a question, what you found. You can attach files to any message.

Mentioning people

  • @name — notifies that person. Use it to pull the right colleague in, rather than writing into the void.
  • @name! (with an exclamation mark) — a help request: a stronger, higher-priority ping when you actually need someone to step in.

Marking the solution

When a message describes how the problem was fixed, mark it as the solution. This flags the answer for next time and automatically moves the trace to Resolved. (If the trace still has open sub-tasks or blockers, the solution is recorded and it resolves once those are done.) See Trace — The lifecycle.

Waiting for something

When a trace is paused waiting on a part, a reply or a delivery, record what you're waiting for. The trace moves to the Waiting column so it's clear it's not abandoned — just blocked on the outside world. When what you were waiting for arrives, mark it arrived and carry on.

Checklist

A simple list of steps to work through. Add items, tick them off, and the card shows the progress (☑ 2/4). It's perfect for a repeatable procedure — "check fuse, clean lens, reseat cable, test".

When a step is really its own job — "order a new part" — you can promote it into a sub-task: its own trace, linked back to this one. See Trace — Sub-tasks & dependencies.

Cost

Record what the fix costs — parts, consumables, labour, external service — with a quantity and a unit cost. The total shows on the card. It's a small habit that, over time, answers a big question: which machines are quietly costing the most?

Members

Add the people involved in the trace. Members stay in the loop and make it clear later who worked on what. (Being a member is about collaboration and notifications — it is separate from the single assignee, who is the one person responsible.)

Good habits

  • Always set the scope and entity — the context is the whole point.
  • Write what you tried, even when it failed — it saves the next person, and makes any escalation far stronger.
  • Mark the solution — one click, it resolves the trace and builds a memory of how you fixed it.
  • Mention the right person instead of hoping someone notices.
