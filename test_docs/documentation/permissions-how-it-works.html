How the application decides, for one specific person, exactly which areas they can open — starting from their role, adjusted by the company, then fine-tuned per person.

New to this? Read Permissions — Overview first for what an area and a level are. This page is about how they get assigned.

It's built in layers

Nobody's access is typed out one permission at a time. It's assembled in four layers, each building on the one before:

  1. The role default. Every user has a type — Manager, Local Manager, IT Support, Accounting, Reservation, Customer Service, and so on. Each type comes with a ready-made bundle of permissions: the sensible starting set for that job.
  2. The company override. A company (owner) can adjust what a type gets for them — “our Managers also handle Marketing.” This replaces the standard bundle for that one type, in that one company.
  3. Per-person grants and revokes. On an individual's page you can grant an extra area they wouldn't normally have, or revoke one they normally would. This is the fine-tuning layer.
  4. Implied permissions. Finally, the “contains” rules fill in the rest automatically: Manage adds View, a whole Location adds each of its tabs, “all reports” adds each report.

Put simply:

Effective access = the role's bundle (or the company's version of it) + anything granted to the person − anything revoked from them, then + everything those imply.

Grant and revoke — the per-person layer

Most people never need this — their role bundle is the whole story. You reach for grant/revoke when one person is an exception:

  • Grant gives an area this person's role wouldn't include — e.g. letting one trusted shop lead see a company report.
  • Revoke takes away an area the role normally includes — e.g. a manager who should not touch pricing.

A revoke always wins: if a person's role grants an area but you've revoked it from them, they don't have it. That's what makes revoke a dependable way to lock one person out of one thing without redesigning the role.

Prefer fixing the role over patching people. If you find yourself granting the same extra area to five people, that's a sign their role default (or the company override) should include it. Per-person grants are for genuine exceptions, not for rebuilding a role one user at a time.

Some access is scoped to which locations

Having an area is one question; where it applies is another. A Local Manager with the Locations area doesn't manage every location — only the ones assigned to them. The permission opens the screen; the assignment decides which rows they see. Fiscal years work the same way: whoever manages Organizations can reach them, but only for the organizations they're allowed to see.

Two roles that always have everything

Two special types are never limited, on purpose:

  • System — the built-in superuser. Holds every permission, always.
  • Integration (API) — the account other systems connect through. It's held to a “has everything” floor so an integration can never be accidentally locked out of a call it depends on.

Why the floor matters. People come and go and get their access trimmed; a live integration must not break when that happens. Guaranteeing the integration account always passes every check keeps connected systems stable no matter how tightly you lock down everyone else.

Where each layer lives

  • Role defaults are the built-in starting bundles — see Permissions — The default starting point.
  • Company overrides are managed by System users on the owner-defaults screen.
  • Grants and revokes live on each person's user page.

When someone “can't get into a screen they should,” walk the layers in order: does their role include the area? Has the company changed that role? Is there a revoke on the person? Nine times out of ten the answer is one of those three.
