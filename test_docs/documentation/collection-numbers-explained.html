Exactly how the Expected cash, the deviation, and the coin-in figure on a collection are worked out — and why there are three different numbers in the first place.

Introduction

A collection carries three figures for the same money: Expected, Cash, and Coin-in. They come from three completely independent places, which is the whole point — if they agree, the collection is honest; if they don't, the gap tells you where to look. This guide explains where each number comes from, with the actual arithmetic.

The three sources:

  • Expected comes from the machine's mechanical counter (a meter of plays).
  • Cash comes from a person counting the money.
  • Coin-in comes from the electronic coin log (each coin reported over the wire).

The counter, and the coefficient

Counter — every machine has a meter that only ever goes up. It counts plays (or pulses) for the machine's whole life. A single reading on its own means nothing; what matters is how much it moved since the last collection.

Counter coefficient — the value of one play. It converts a number of plays into money. If a play costs 0.50, the coefficient is 0.50.

Why the difference, not the reading: a machine's counter might read 480,000. That number is meaningless by itself. Subtract the previous collection's reading and you get the plays since you last emptied it — which is exactly the period the Expected cash should cover.

The Expected cash formula

Putting it together, this is precisely how Expected cash is computed:

counter plays   = counter now − counter at last collection

Expected cash = counter plays × counter coefficient

In words: the cash plays since the last collection, multiplied by the value of a play.

Cashless (Nayax) plays don't need removing. The counter only advances on cash plays — a cashless play through a Nayax reader does not spin it. So the plays it counts are already cash-only, and nothing has to be subtracted. (Older machines whose counter also advanced on cashless plays needed that correction; current pulseboxes don't, so it no longer applies.)

Never negative. If the readings would produce a negative result (a counter that appears to have gone backwards, a reset, or a mis-typed reading), Expected is clamped to 0 rather than shown as a negative number. A machine showing 0 expected when you know it earned is a sign the counter readings need checking.

A worked example

Say a machine at 0.50 per play:

  • Counter now 10,500, last collection 10,000 → 500 cash plays.
  • Expected cash = 500 × 0.50 = 250.

If the person then counts 245 in the box, the collection is 5 short of expected — about 2%. Small and probably fine. A gap of 50 on the same collection would be worth a closer look.

Cash, and the deviation

Cash is the simplest of the three — it's the amount a person physically counted and entered. No calculation.

The deviation is just the difference between what was counted and what the counter expected:

deviation   = cash counted − expected cash
deviation % = deviation ÷ expected cash × 100

A negative deviation means less cash than the counter expected — the direction that matters. A small percentage is normal; a large or repeating one is the signal.

Coin-in — the third, independent figure

Everything above comes from the mechanical counter. Coin-in comes from somewhere else entirely: the electronic log the acceptor produces every time a coin drops, each coin already carrying its configured value.

Which coins belong to the collection: the coins logged between the previous collection's confirmation and this one's. Because those confirmation moments are exact timestamps, the coin-in figure lines up with the counter's window precisely — no guessing which day a coin counts for.

So a collection is checked three ways from three independent origins: the meter says Expected, the person says Cash, the coin log says Coin-in. A counter that has drifted shows up as Expected disagreeing with Coin-in; money going missing shows up as Cash below both.

When Coin-in reads 0 but the machine clearly earned: its coins were logged before that machine's coin values were configured, so they have no known value. Those coins are counted but worth an unknown amount — a data gap, not a shortfall. It only affects the older, pre-configuration coins.

In summary

Expected = counter plays × value per play. Cash = what was counted. Coin-in = the electronic coin log over the same window. Three origins, one truth — and the gaps between them are where the answers live.
