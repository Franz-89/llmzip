How the money a coin acceptor takes in travels from the machine to your accounts — and which report answers which question.

Introduction

Every time a coin goes into an acceptor, the machine reports it electronically. That electronic record is the backbone of the coin reports. This guide explains the lifecycle of that money — from the moment a coin is logged to the moment it is counted at collection — so that the numbers on the different reports make sense and never surprise you.

The one idea to take away: a coin passes through three stages — logged, then in the box, then counted. Each report looks at a different stage. Once you know which stage a report is about, the numbers always add up.

Base concepts

Coin-in (the log) — The electronic record of coins the acceptor reported. Each coin carries the value configured for its pin (its money value), stamped at the moment it went in. This is the raw truth of what the machine received.

Collection — When a person physically empties a machine, reads its counter, and counts the cash. A collection is confirmed the moment its counter reading is approved — that instant is what ties the electronic log to the physical count.

Counted vs uncounted — Coins logged before the last confirmed collection have already been counted. Coins logged after it are still in the box, waiting to be collected.

Unpriced coins. Coins logged before a machine's pin values were configured have no stamped value. They are still counted as coins, but their money reads as 0 because we genuinely don't know what they were worth. Every report shows these separately as "unpriced" so a €0 is never mistaken for an empty box or a shortfall. This only affects the historical tail — every new coin carries its value.

Invalid coins. A faulty pulse line can log dozens of "coins" in under a second — physically impossible. A background check flags those as invalid and removes them from every total, so a wiring fault never inflates your figures. A machine that does this repeatedly is surfaced as a likely wiring problem.

The three stages, and the number each report shows

Take one machine over a period. Its coin-in splits cleanly:

Total logged = already counted + still in the box

For example, a machine that logged €44 in the period, whose last collection was confirmed part-way through, might be €17.50 already counted and €26.50 still in the box. The two always add back up to the total.

Coin-in report — the total logged over a date range. Everything the acceptors reported, by denomination, by game, by location, plus when the coins came in (a day-by-hour heatmap and a daily trend). This is your analysis view: which games and times pull the most cash.

Cash in Box report — the still-in-the-box amount, right now, per machine. Not a date range — a live snapshot of money waiting to be collected. This is the operational view: where to send someone to collect.

Coin Reconciliation report — the counted slice, checked three ways. For each confirmed collection it lines up the counter meter's expected cash, the cash actually counted, and the coin-in log for that exact collection window. Gaps between them are the signal — money missing between the box and the count.

Why the same machine can show different numbers

This is the question that trips people up, and the answer is always the same: the reports are looking at different stages.

  • The Coin-in report shows the whole period's total — counted and uncounted together.
  • The Cash in Box report shows only what is still in the box (logged since the last collection).
  • The Reconciliation report shows only what a confirmed collection has already captured.

A number that seems "missing" is usually just in a different stage. When you confirm a new collection, the money that was "in the box" moves into "counted" — it disappears from Cash in Box and appears in Reconciliation. Nothing is lost; it simply crossed a stage.

Collection thresholds

Because Cash in Box knows exactly how much is waiting in every machine, it can warn you before a box fills up or a game gets blocked. You set a threshold per shop and an optional cap per machine; when a shop or machine goes over, the people responsible are notified so they can collect in time. Setting this up is covered in its own guide.

In short: the Coin-in report is for analysis, Cash in Box is for knowing what to collect now, and Reconciliation is for checking the count is honest. Same money, three questions.
