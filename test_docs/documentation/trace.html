Trace is your shared, internal workspace for anything that needs attention or action — a fault, a complaint, a task, an investigation — attached to the exact thing it is about, and driven all the way to "done".

What Trace is for

Every day, things come up that someone needs to follow through: a bill acceptor jams, a customer disputes a charge, a kiosk needs a part, a payment terminal goes offline, a cash count doesn't add up. Trace is where all of that lives — one shared board the whole team can see, instead of messages scattered across chats, notes and memory.

Two ideas make Trace different from a plain to-do list:

  • Context. A trace is never floating — it is attached to the thing it is about (a specific game, NUC, kiosk, terminal, reservation, customer, location…). Anyone opening it immediately knows what and where, with no extra explaining.
  • It is a complete record. Everything that happens — comments, status changes, costs, who was involved, the solution — is captured in one place, in order. When you look back in six months, the whole story is there.

Trace is internal. It is a tool for your team — customers never see it. It is the place where work is organised, tracked, and, when you get stuck, escalated to SmartMonkey for help (more on that at the end).

If you've used Trello or Jira, this will feel familiar.

A board, cards, statuses, comments — the shape is the same, and that's deliberate. But Trace is not meant to replace them. Although it is capable across a wide range of work, it is built for one specific job: to close the gap between the shops and the support team. That's why every trace carries the real context of which machine, terminal or location it's about, and can be escalated straight to SmartMonkey when needed. You're welcome to use it more broadly for everyday work — just know that the shop–to–support flow is what it's designed around.

One system, every user

Trace is fully integrated into both SapiensCore and SapiensClient. It is not a separate app you have to remember to open — it is part of the platform your team already uses, in the place where they already work.

Because of that, it natively supports everyone:

  • Managers who work from the cloud — in SapiensCore;
  • Employees who work in the shop — in SapiensClient, on the SapiensWorkstation.

As a consequence, Trace is user-based by nature: every trace, comment and change is tied to the real person who did it, wherever they did it.

On the shop floor. An employee working at location A can open Trace right on the SapiensWorkstation — see the board, receive updates, and create traces — with no special login. Because they are physically in the shop, everything they do is automatically marked as coming from that shop and that user. The context is filled in for them.

Base concepts

A few terms come up constantly. Make sure they are clear before you start.

Trace — A single thing to follow through. It has a title, a description, a status, and a unique number (for example #312) you can use to refer to it.

Scope & entity (the context) — The scope is the kind of thing the trace is about (Game, NUC, Kiosk, POS Terminal, Reservation, Customer, Location, Cash…); the entity is the specific one (this game, this terminal). Together they are the trace's context.

Status — Where the trace is in its life: Open, In Progress, Waiting, Resolved, Closed. These are the columns on the board.

Priority — How urgent: Low, Normal, High, Critical. It is a visual cue, shown as a coloured badge and a coloured strip on the card.

Effort — A rough estimate of how much work the trace is (separate from how urgent it is). Picked from a fixed scale — see Estimating effort below.

Assignee & Members — The assignee is the one person responsible. Members are everyone else involved (for collaboration and notifications).

"Resolved" and "Closed" are not the same thing. Resolved means the work is done but the trace stays on the board for a while, so it can be checked and easily reopened if the problem comes back. Closed means archived — off the board, but always findable. You rarely close a trace by hand; the system does it for you. This is explained fully in The lifecycle.

The board

The board is the main screen: four columns — Open, In Progress, Waiting, Resolved — each holding cards. Each card is one trace.

Reading a card at a glance

A card is designed so you can understand a trace without opening it. On it you will find:

  • the scope (e.g. 🎮 Game, 🖥️ Kiosk) and the entity / location it concerns;
  • the title and the trace #number;
  • the priority badge and a matching coloured strip down the left edge;
  • the effort estimate and the due date, if set;
  • small markers for activity: 💬 comments, 📎 attachments, a cost total, ☑ checklist progress;
  • relationship signals: 🔒 Blocked (it is waiting on something else), 🌿 sub-tasks (1/3 done), 🔗 links, and ↻ if it has been reopened before.

Moving and ordering cards

Change status: drag a card from one column to another — e.g. from Open to In Progress when you start working it.

Set the order within a column: drag a card up or down inside its column to arrange it. The order is shared — everyone sees the same board — so the simple rule is: the top card is the next one to do.

Priority is a label, not the order. You decide the order by dragging. If a column gets messy, the small "sort by priority" button in the column header re-orders it from most to least urgent in one click.

Finding things

The toolbar above the board lets you filter by location, assignee, priority, type, overdue, and search by text. To jump straight to a specific trace, type its number into "Go to #" and press Enter.

You can also switch between the Board (cards) and the List (a dense table) using the toggle in the toolbar. The List view is also where you browse resolved and closed traces — see Looking back.

Creating a trace

Use New to open the create form. It walks you through a few short steps:

  1. What is it about? — Pick the scope (Game, Kiosk, Terminal, Reservation, Customer, Cash, Other…).
  2. Which one? — Pick the specific entity. For things tied to a location (a game, a NUC, a kiosk) choose the location first to narrow the list; for others (a customer, a payment) just start typing to search. Some scopes (Cash, Other, Location) need no entity.
  3. Describe it — A short, clear title and, if useful, a longer description.
  4. Classify — The type (Maintenance, Incident, Complaint, Request, Fraud, Other), the priority, and — for hardware — an operational status (Operational / Degraded / Offline).
  5. Who & when — Optionally an assignee, a due date, and an effort estimate.
  6. Optional — A starting checklist and any files (photos of the fault, documents…).

A good title saves everyone time. "Bill acceptor jams on coin 2" is far more useful at a glance than "machine broken". The context (which game, which shop) is already attached, so the title can focus on the problem.

Working a trace — the detail view

Click a card to open it. The top of the detail view shows the #number, the title, and a row of controls to set status, priority, assignee, due date and effort — change any of them right there.

Just under that, a relationship bar appears when the trace is connected to others: its parent (Part of #n), what is blocking it, what it blocks, and its sub-tasks. Each number is clickable, so you can hop between connected traces.

Below, the work itself is organised into tabs.

Timeline (comments & activity)

The Timeline is the conversation and the history together — comments you write, plus automatic entries for things like status changes. The newest entry is at the top.

  • Write a message to record progress, ask a question, or note what you found.
  • Mention a colleague with @name to notify them. Use @name! (with an exclamation mark) for a help request — a stronger, higher-priority ping.
  • Attach files — photos and documents — to any message.
  • "Waiting for" — when a trace is paused waiting on something (a part, a reply), record what you're waiting on; the trace moves to the Waiting column.

Marking the solution. When a message explains how the problem was fixed, mark it as the solution. This does two things: it flags the answer for next time, and it automatically moves the trace to Resolved. (If the trace still has open sub-tasks or blockers, the solution is recorded and it resolves once those are done.)

Checklist

A simple list of steps to work through — tick them off as you go. The card shows the progress (e.g. ☑ 2/4).

Promote a step into its own trace. Sometimes a checklist step turns out to be real work of its own (e.g. "order a new lens"). Use promote on that item and it becomes a sub-task — its own trace, with its own assignee, effort and timeline — linked back to this one. The step now shows as "→ #n", and the parent cannot be closed until the sub-task is done.

Cost

Record the cost of fixing the trace — parts, consumables, labour, external service. The total appears on the card. Over time this is what tells you which machines are quietly costing you the most.

Members

Add the people involved. Members are kept in the loop and make it clear, later, who worked on what.

Related

The full picture of how this trace connects to others. The goal is bigger than tidy links: connecting traces turns a pile of tickets into the memory of your operation — cause and effect, duplicates caught, and recurring problems on the same machine made obvious.

  • Sub-tasks — children promoted from the checklist, with their progress.
  • Part of — the parent, if this trace is itself a sub-task.
  • Blocked by / Blocks — dependencies on other traces.
  • Linked traces — meaningful connections you add by hand: Caused by, Duplicate, Blocks.
  • Other traces on this entity — everything else recorded against the same game/terminal/customer, so recurring problems stand out.

Full detail in Trace — Related traces, sub-tasks & dependencies.

Blocking and the close-gate. If trace A is blocked by trace B, A cannot be moved to Resolved/Closed until B is done. The board shows a 🔒 Blocked chip on any trace that is currently waiting on an open dependency, so you can see at a glance what is stuck.

Estimating effort

Effort answers "roughly how much work is this?" — which is different from how urgent it is. It is chosen from a fixed scale, not typed in freely:

30 min · 1h · 2h · Half day · 1 day · 2–3 days · 1 week · 2 weeks · 1 month

The scale is deliberately coarse at the top: nobody can tell a 31-hour job from a 34-hour job, so beyond a point you simply round to the next honest bucket. Pick the closest one and move on — the value is in the rough comparison across traces, not in false precision.

The lifecycle — from open to archived

A trace flows through a natural cycle. Most of it happens on its own; you mostly just do the work.

  1. Open → In Progress — you pick it up and work it (drag it across, or change the status).
  2. Waiting — if you get stuck waiting on something, park it here with a "waiting for" note.
  3. Resolved — mark the solution (or move it yourself). The trace stays on the board for a grace period so it can be checked or reopened if the fix doesn't hold.
  4. Closed (archived) — after the grace period, the system closes it automatically. It leaves the board but is never lost — you can always find it in the List view.

Why the grace period? A "fixed" machine that breaks again two days later isn't really fixed. Keeping resolved traces on the board for a while — then archiving them automatically — means the board stays clean without anyone having to tidy it, and genuine fixes get a chance to prove themselves first.

Reopening

If a problem comes back, open the resolved/closed trace and use Reopen. It goes back to Open, and the system keeps count: a trace that has been reopened shows a ↻ ×n marker. A high reopen count is a useful warning sign — a recurring, never-quite-fixed problem.

Looking back — the List & Archive

Switch to the List view (the toggle in the toolbar) for a dense, sortable table of traces. At the top you choose what to show:

  • Active — the same set as the board.
  • Resolved / Closed / All — the archive. Add a date range to answer questions like "what did we resolve at this shop last month?"

The same filters as the board (location, assignee, priority, type) apply here too. This is how you find a closed trace again — or just say to a colleague, "look at Trace 312", and they type 312 into Go to #.

Escalating to SmartMonkey

When something is beyond what the team can resolve on its own — you need a configuration changed, an authorisation, a deeper investigation — you can escalate the trace to SmartMonkey. This pushes the trace, with its full context and history, to the SmartMonkey support team, who can then work on it directly.

You'll find Escalate to SmartMonkey in the trace's escalation strip. Add a short note describing exactly what you need, and send. After that, the trace shows the SmartMonkey state at a glance:

  • Escalated — sent, waiting for SmartMonkey to pick it up.
  • Reviewing — someone at SmartMonkey has taken it.
  • SM done — their part is finished (your trace can then carry on or be closed).

Who can escalate. Escalation is controlled by a permission. SmartMonkey staff have it by default; for your own team it is granted per user, so you decide who is allowed to escalate. If you don't see the option, you don't currently have the permission — ask your manager.

Before you escalate, make the trace tell the story. The strength of an escalation is the context that travels with it — the entity it's attached to, what you've already tried (in the timeline), photos, the exact thing you need. A well-filled trace gets a fast, accurate answer; a one-line trace gets questions back.

Full guide: for exactly how escalation works, who is allowed to do it, and the status flow end to end, see Trace — Escalating to SmartMonkey.

Notifications

Trace keeps people informed without anyone having to chase. You receive a notification (in the browser, and on Telegram if linked) when you are mentioned, assigned a trace, or sent a help request (@name!), among others. This is why mentioning the right person, rather than just writing into the void, matters.

Good habits

  • One trace, one problem. If a step grows into separate work, promote it to a sub-task rather than overloading the original.
  • Keep the timeline honest. Note what you tried, even when it didn't work — it saves the next person from repeating it, and makes any escalation far stronger.
  • Mark the solution. It's one click, it resolves the trace, and it builds a memory of "how we fixed this last time".
  • Order is a promise. The top card is the next job — keep your columns ordered so the board means something.
  • Attach the context. Always pick the right scope and entity. A trace without context is just a sticky note.
