Real work is rarely one isolated job. This guide covers how traces connect — breaking a big job into sub-tasks, and handling traces that block one another.

New to Trace? See Trace — Overview & how to use it and Trace — Creating & working a trace first.

Why connect traces — the goal

On their own, traces are a to-do list. Connected, they become a picture of how your operation actually behaves. That's the whole point of relating traces, and it does three things for you:

  • It shows cause and effect. "This outage was caused by that change." Linking the two means the next person sees the chain — not two unrelated tickets they have to piece together.
  • It avoids duplicated effort. Marking a trace as a duplicate of another keeps the work, and the conversation, in one place — instead of two people fixing the same thing twice.
  • It reveals patterns. Because every trace stays attached to its entity, the same machine's whole history sits together. A game with eight traces this year isn't eight isolated faults — it's a machine telling you something.

And breaking a big job into sub-tasks keeps each piece honest: nothing gets marked "done" while a real part of it is still open.

In one line: relating traces turns a pile of tickets into the memory of your operation — what broke, what caused it, what fixed it, and what keeps coming back.

The relationship bar

When a trace is connected to others, a relationship bar appears at the top of its detail view — the quick map of how it fits in:

  • Part of #n — this trace is a sub-task of another.
  • Blocked by — traces that must be done before this one can close (with a dot showing if each is still open or done).
  • Blocks — traces this one is holding up.
  • Sub-tasks — the children of this trace, and their progress.

Every number in the bar is clickable, so you can hop straight between connected traces.

Sub-tasks — splitting a job

Sometimes what looked like a single step turns into real work of its own. Instead of overloading the original trace, you promote that step into a sub-task: its own trace, with its own assignee, effort, timeline and history — linked back to the parent.

How to promote a step

  1. Open the trace and go to the Checklist tab.
  2. On the step you want to split out, use promote.
  3. A new trace is created from that step — it inherits the parent's context (the same entity, location and priority), so you don't re-enter it — and it's linked to the parent.

The checklist step now shows as "→ #n" — handed off — and the new sub-task appears under Sub-tasks on the parent. You then give the sub-task its own assignee and effort and work it like any trace.

The parent waits for its children. A parent trace cannot be resolved or closed while it still has an open sub-task. This is deliberate: it stops a job being marked "done" while a real piece of it is still outstanding.

When to promote vs keep one trace. Keep it as a checklist step if it's a quick action in the same flow. Promote it to a sub-task when it has its own life — a different owner, a different timeline, something you'll track separately (ordering a part, waiting on an external service). One trace, one problem.

Blocking — "this can't be done until that is"

Traces can depend on each other. If trace A is blocked by trace B, then A is genuinely stuck until B is finished — and Trace enforces that.

  • The relationship bar and the Related tab show Blocked by and Blocks, each with a status dot.
  • On the board, any trace currently waiting on an open dependency carries a 🔒 Blocked chip — so you can see at a glance what is stuck and why.

The close-gate. A blocked trace cannot be moved to Resolved or Closed while any of its blockers (or open sub-tasks) are still open. If you try, it won't let you. This keeps the board honest — nothing is marked done while something it depends on is still hanging.

Links — connecting related traces

Beyond parent/child and blocking, you can connect traces that are simply related, from the Related tab:

  • Caused by — this problem was caused by another trace.
  • Duplicate — this is the same issue as another trace.
  • Blocks — mark a blocking dependency by hand.

To add one, open Add link, choose the type, and find the other trace by its #number or title.

Other traces on this entity (automatic)

You don't have to link these by hand. The Related tab shows, automatically, every other trace recorded against the same entity — the same game, terminal or customer. This is the system relating traces for you, and it's where recurring problems reveal themselves: if a machine has five traces this year you'll see them all in one place — the difference between "another fault" and "this machine again".

In short

  • Promote a checklist step into a sub-task when it deserves its own trace — the parent then waits for it.
  • Blocked-by means it can't close until the blocker is done; the board flags it with 🔒.
  • Use links (caused-by / duplicate) to connect related traces, and check other traces on this entity to spot patterns.
